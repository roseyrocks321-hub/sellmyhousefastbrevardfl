Selling a House As-Is in Brevard County: What Cash Buyers Look For

Not every home on the market is a picture-perfect listing ready for Instagram. Some houses have leaky roofs. Others have kitchens frozen in the 1970s. And in Brevard County, where salt air, tropical storms, and aging construction take their toll, plenty of properties need work — sometimes a lot of it.

If you own a home in Melbourne, Palm Bay, Cocoa Beach, or anywhere along the Space Coast and you're considering selling without making repairs, you're looking at what's known as an as-is sale. This guide explains what "as-is" really means, why cash buyers are the ideal audience for these properties, and how to ensure you get a fair offer — even if your house has seen better days.

What Does "As-Is" Really Mean?

When you sell a house as-is, you're telling buyers that you will not make any repairs, improvements, or concessions before closing. The buyer purchases the property in its current condition, with all its flaws — whether that's a broken HVAC system, a damaged roof, outdated electrical wiring, or simply years of cosmetic wear and tear.

Importantly, selling as-is does not relieve you of your legal obligation to disclose known defects. Under Florida law, sellers must disclose any material facts that could affect the property's value or desirability. This includes things like:

  • Known mold or water damage
  • Foundation or structural issues
  • Termite or pest infestations
  • Previous flooding or hurricane damage
  • Code violations or unpermitted work
  • Environmental hazards like lead paint or asbestos

Transparency protects you from future legal liability and builds trust with buyers — particularly cash buyers who are experienced in evaluating distressed properties.

Common Reasons Brevard County Homeowners Sell As-Is

Every as-is sale has a story. Over the years, we've worked with homeowners across Brevard County facing a wide range of situations that make repairing a home impractical or impossible.

Storm and Hurricane Damage

Living on Florida's Space Coast means dealing with hurricane season every year. Even if your home survived the last major storm without catastrophic damage, wind and water can cause hidden problems — roof leaks, compromised siding, flooding in crawl spaces — that are expensive to fully remediate. Homes in low-lying areas of Merritt Island, Cocoa Beach, and Satellite Beach are especially vulnerable.

Outdated or Functionally Obsolete Homes

Many homes in Brevard County were built during the Space Race era of the 1960s and 1970s. While these homes have character, they often lack modern amenities that today's buyers expect. Galvanized plumbing, aluminum wiring, small closets, and closed-off floor plans can make a home difficult to sell on the traditional market without major renovations.

Code Violations and Unpermitted Work

Previous owners — or even the current owner — may have added rooms, converted garages, or installed pools without pulling the proper permits. When these issues are discovered during a traditional sale, they can derail the transaction or require costly retroactive permitting. Cash buyers are often willing to take on these headaches.

Financial Hardship

Sometimes the home needs repairs, but the owner's bank account is empty. A new roof can cost $15,000 to $30,000 in Brevard County. A full kitchen remodel can easily exceed $40,000. For homeowners facing job loss, medical bills, or other financial strain, selling as-is is the only viable option.

Inherited or Unwanted Rental Properties

Heirs who inherit homes in Titusville, Rockledge, or Viera often discover that the property hasn't been updated in decades. Similarly, landlords who are tired of dealing with tenants and maintenance may choose to offload a rental property as-is rather than invest in renovations.

Fire, Mold, or Structural Damage

Severe damage from house fires, long-term mold growth, or foundation settling can render a home uninhabitable or uninsurable. Traditional lenders typically won't finance these properties, which limits the buyer pool to cash investors.

How Cash Buyers Evaluate As-Is Properties

If you're selling as-is, cash buyers will likely be your primary audience. But how do they decide what your home is worth in its current condition? Understanding their evaluation process helps you set realistic expectations and recognize a fair offer when you see one.

After Repair Value (ARV)

The first number a cash buyer calculates is the ARV — what the home would sell for after all repairs and renovations are complete. To determine ARV, buyers analyze comparable sales of similar homes in your neighborhood. A 1,500-square-foot home in Palm Bay with three bedrooms and two baths will be compared to other recently sold 3/2 homes in Palm Bay that were in move-in-ready condition.

Repair and Renovation Costs

Next, the buyer estimates the cost of bringing the home to market condition. This includes:

  • Structural repairs (foundation, roof, framing)
  • Mechanical systems (HVAC, plumbing, electrical)
  • Cosmetic updates (flooring, paint, cabinets, countertops)
  • Exterior work (landscaping, siding, driveway, pool repairs)
  • Code compliance and permit costs
  • Contingency reserves for unexpected issues

In Brevard County, where labor costs and material prices have risen steadily, repair estimates can be substantial — especially for homes near the coast that require storm-resistant upgrades.

Holding and Selling Costs

Cash buyers also factor in the costs they'll incur between purchase and resale. These include property taxes, insurance, utilities, lawn maintenance, and interest on borrowed capital. When they eventually sell the renovated home, they'll pay realtor commissions (5–6%), closing costs, and transfer taxes. These expenses typically total 10–15% of the ARV.

Profit Margin

Finally, the buyer builds in a profit margin to compensate for their risk, time, and capital. For a full gut renovation, investors typically target a 15–20% margin. For lighter cosmetic projects, the margin may be 10–15%.

What This Means for Your Offer

Putting it all together, a cash offer on an as-is property typically falls in the range of 50–70% of the ARV, depending on the scope of repairs and current market conditions. That may sound low, but remember: you're saving tens of thousands in repair costs, months of holding expenses, and 5–6% in agent commissions.

The Real Cost of Repairs: A Brevard County Breakdown

To put repair costs in perspective, here are typical price ranges for common projects in the Space Coast area:

  • Roof replacement: $12,000 – $28,000 (higher for tile or metal)
  • HVAC system: $6,000 – $12,000
  • Elect panel upgrade: $2,500 – $5,000
  • Full kitchen remodel: $25,000 – $60,000
  • Bathroom remodel: $10,000 – $25,000 per bath
  • Foundation repairs: $5,000 – $25,000+
  • Mold remediation: $2,000 – $15,000+
  • Pool resurfacing/repair: $5,000 – $15,000
  • Impact windows (hurricane protection): $8,000 – $25,000

For a home needing $50,000 to $75,000 in repairs, a cash offer in the 50–60% range of ARV can actually net you close to what you'd receive after repairs and commissions on the open market — without any of the work, stress, or uncertainty.

What Cash Buyers Want to See

Even though cash buyers purchase as-is, certain factors make a property more attractive — and can lead to a higher offer:

  • Good location: Homes in desirable areas like Viera, Satellite Beach, or Suntree command higher ARVs, which means more room for a competitive offer.
  • Solid bones: A home with good structural integrity and a functional floor plan is easier to renovate than one with foundation issues or an awkward layout.
  • Clear title: Cash buyers can handle liens and title issues, but a clean title speeds up closing and reduces risk — both of which can improve your offer.
  • Reasonable lot size: In Brevard County, homes on larger lots or with water access have higher upside potential for investors.
  • Honest disclosure: Buyers appreciate transparency. If you're upfront about defects, the evaluation process goes smoother and trust is established.

Red Flags: Avoiding Bad Actors in the As-Is Market

Unfortunately, not every "We Buy Houses" operation is ethical. Protect yourself by watching for these warning signs:

  • Offers made sight unseen: A legitimate buyer will want to see the property or at least review detailed photos before making an offer. Be cautious of anyone who quotes a price without any information.
  • Upfront fees: Never pay for an "inspection," "application," or "processing" fee. Legitimate buyers don't charge sellers.
  • Pressure to sign immediately: A fair offer will still be there in 24 or 48 hours. If someone demands an immediate signature, walk away.
  • Earnest money that never materializes: A serious buyer will put earnest money into escrow. If they refuse, that's a red flag.
  • Last-minute price reductions: Some unscrupulous buyers offer a high price to get you under contract, then reduce it at the last minute claiming "unexpected repairs." Work with buyers who stand by their initial offer.
