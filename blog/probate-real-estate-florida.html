Probate Real Estate in Florida: A Guide for Heirs and Executors

Losing a loved one is never easy, and when real estate is part of their estate, the situation can become even more complicated. If you've inherited property in Brevard County — whether it's a beach cottage in Cocoa Beach, a ranch home in Melbourne, or a condo in Palm Bay — you'll likely need to navigate the Florida probate process before you can sell or transfer the property.

This guide breaks down everything heirs and executors need to know about probate real estate in Florida. From understanding the legal timeline to exploring your selling options, we'll help you make informed decisions during a difficult time.

What Is Probate Real Estate?

Probate real estate refers to property that is part of a deceased person's estate and must go through the probate court process before it can be sold or transferred to heirs. In Florida, probate ensures that debts are paid, taxes are settled, and remaining assets are distributed according to the will — or, if there is no will, according to state intestacy laws.

Not all real estate requires probate. Property held in a living trust, jointly owned with rights of survivorship, or with designated beneficiaries (like a transfer-on-death deed) typically bypasses probate entirely. However, if the deceased was the sole owner or owned the property as a tenant in common, probate is usually necessary.

Why Probate Matters for Heirs in Brevard County

Brevard County's real estate market is unique. With properties ranging from oceanfront estates in Satellite Beach to family homes in Rockledge and Titusville, inherited real estate can represent significant value — but also significant responsibility. Until probate is complete, heirs generally cannot sell, refinance, or make major decisions about the property.

The Florida Probate Process: A Step-by-Step Overview

Florida's probate system is administered through the circuit courts. For Brevard County residents, probate cases are filed with the 18th Judicial Circuit Court in Viera. Here's how the process typically unfolds:

Step 1: File the Petition

The probate process begins when an interested party — usually the executor named in the will or a close family member — files a petition with the court. If there is a valid will, the court will validate it and officially appoint the personal representative (executor). If there is no will, the court will appoint an administrator according to Florida's priority rules, typically starting with the surviving spouse.

Step 2: Notify Creditors and Heirs

The personal representative must notify all known creditors of the estate and publish a notice to creditors in a local newspaper. In Brevard County, this is typically published in Florida Today or another court-approved publication. Creditors generally have three months from the date of first publication to file claims against the estate.

Step 3: Inventory the Estate

The personal representative must create a detailed inventory of all estate assets, including real estate, bank accounts, investments, and personal property. For real estate, this often requires a professional appraisal to determine the fair market value as of the date of death.

Step 4: Pay Debts and Taxes

Before any assets can be distributed to heirs, the estate must pay valid creditor claims, funeral expenses, and any applicable taxes. This includes federal estate taxes (for estates over the exemption threshold), Florida estate taxes (currently none), and final income taxes for the deceased.

Step 5: Distribute Remaining Assets

Once debts and taxes are settled, the personal representative petitions the court for permission to distribute the remaining assets. If the will specifies who receives the property, the court will order distribution accordingly. If there is no will, Florida's intestacy laws determine the heirs — typically the surviving spouse and children, or more distant relatives if neither exists.

Formal vs. Summary Administration in Florida

Florida offers two main types of probate: formal administration and summary administration. The type required depends on the value of the estate and how long the decedent has been deceased.

Summary Administration

Summary administration is a streamlined probate process available when:

  • The total value of the probate estate is less than $75,000, or
  • The decedent has been deceased for more than two years.

Because summary administration skips many of the steps required in formal administration — such as appointing a personal representative and filing ongoing reports — it is typically faster and less expensive. Heirs can often complete summary administration in a few months, making it an attractive option for smaller estates or cases where the death occurred years ago.

Formal Administration

Formal administration is required for larger estates and most cases where the decedent has been deceased for less than two years. This is the standard probate process and includes full court oversight, creditor notification periods, and detailed accounting requirements. For Brevard County estates with significant real estate holdings, formal administration is usually necessary.

Formal administration typically takes 6 to 12 months, though complex cases can take longer. During this time, the property cannot be sold without court approval unless the personal representative has been granted specific authority to do so.

Selling Real Estate During Probate in Florida

One of the most common questions heirs ask is whether they can sell the property while probate is ongoing. The answer depends on the type of probate and the authority granted to the personal representative.

Selling During Formal Administration

In formal administration, the personal representative may have limited or unlimited authority. If the will grants unlimited authority, or if the court issues letters of administration with full powers, the personal representative can sell real estate without a separate court hearing. However, if authority is limited, the representative must petition the court for approval before listing or accepting an offer.

This extra layer of court oversight protects heirs but can also delay the sale. For families eager to settle the estate — especially if the property is vacant, deteriorating, or located in an area with seasonal market fluctuations like Cocoa Beach — these delays can be frustrating.

Selling During Summary Administration

Since summary administration does not appoint a personal representative, any sale of real estate typically requires all heirs to agree and sign the necessary documents. If heirs disagree about whether to sell, the price, or the timing, the case may need to be converted to formal administration to resolve the dispute.

Tax Considerations When Selling Inherited Property

Inherited property benefits from a "stepped-up basis," which means the tax basis is adjusted to the fair market value at the date of death. This can significantly reduce or eliminate capital gains taxes when the property is sold. For example, if your parents purchased their home in Viera for $100,000 and it was worth $350,000 when they passed away, you would only owe capital gains on appreciation above $350,000.

However, property taxes in Brevard County may increase after inheritance if the property loses its homestead exemption. Florida's Save Our Homes cap limits annual increases in assessed value for primary residences, but this protection does not transfer to heirs who do not use the property as their primary residence.

Challenges of Selling Inherited Property in Brevard County

Selling a home you've inherited comes with unique challenges that go beyond the legal complexities of probate.

Emotional Attachment

For many heirs, the inherited property holds decades of memories. Deciding what to do with a childhood home in Rockledge or a grandparents' condo in Cape Canaveral can be emotionally draining. It's important to give yourself time to process your grief while also being realistic about the financial and practical demands of property ownership.

Property Condition

Inherited homes are often older and may require significant repairs. The previous owner may have deferred maintenance due to age, illness, or financial constraints. If the home has been vacant for months — or longer — issues like mold, plumbing leaks, roof damage, or pest infestations are common in Florida's humid climate.

Making repairs before a traditional sale can cost tens of thousands of dollars, with no guarantee that you'll recoup the investment. This is especially true for homes in areas like Palm Bay or Cocoa, where buyers often expect move-in-ready properties.

Multiple Heirs

When several siblings or relatives inherit a property together, disagreements are common. One sibling may want to keep the home as a rental, another may want to renovate and sell, and a third may simply want cash as soon as possible. These conflicts can paralyze decision-making and prolong probate.

Out-of-State Heirs

Many families in Brevard County have adult children living in other states. Managing a probate property from afar — coordinating repairs, overseeing showings, and handling legal paperwork — adds logistical complexity and expense. Out-of-state heirs often prefer a quick, simple sale over a lengthy traditional listing process.

Tips for Executors and Heirs in Brevard County

If you've been named executor or stand to inherit property in Brevard County, these tips can help you navigate the process more smoothly:

  • Secure the property immediately. Change the locks, maintain insurance, and ensure utilities are functional to prevent further damage.
  • Get multiple valuations. Order a professional appraisal for probate purposes, but also get a cash offer or comparative market analysis to understand your real-world selling options.
  • Communicate openly with other heirs. Transparency prevents disputes. Keep all beneficiaries informed about timelines, offers, and decisions.
  • Consult a probate attorney. Florida probate law is complex. An attorney can help you avoid costly mistakes and ensure compliance with court requirements.
  • Consider the total cost of ownership. Factor in property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and mortgage payments (if applicable) when deciding whether to keep or sell the property.
