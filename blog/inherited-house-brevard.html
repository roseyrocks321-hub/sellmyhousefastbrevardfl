Inherited a House in Brevard County? Your Step-by-Step Action Plan

Inheriting a house can feel like both a blessing and a burden. One moment you're grieving the loss of a loved one, and the next you're responsible for a property in Melbourne, Palm Bay, or Cocoa Beach that may need repairs, carry a mortgage, or be tied up in legal proceedings. If you've recently inherited real estate in Brevard County, you're probably wondering: What do I do now?

This step-by-step action plan will help you navigate the first critical decisions, protect the property's value, and choose the best path forward — whether that's moving in, renting it out, or selling fast for cash.

Step 1: Secure the Property Immediately

The first thing you should do after inheriting a home is secure it. Vacant properties attract vandalism, squatters, and weather-related damage — especially during Florida's storm season. Even if the home appears fine on the outside, unseen issues like plumbing leaks or HVAC failures can cause thousands of dollars in damage within weeks.

What to Do Right Away

  • Change the locks. You don't know who has keys — neighbors, caregivers, contractors, or former tenants.
  • Check utilities. Make sure electricity and water are on, or safely shut off if the home will be vacant for an extended period.
  • Verify insurance coverage. Contact the insurance company to confirm whether the property is still covered and for how long. You may need to purchase a vacant dwelling policy.
  • Inspect for damage. Walk through the home and look for roof leaks, mold, pest infestations, or broken windows. Document everything with photos.

Homes in coastal areas like Satellite Beach or Merritt Island are especially vulnerable to moisture intrusion and salt air corrosion. A quick inspection now can prevent costly surprises later.

Step 2: Determine Whether Probate Is Required

Before you can sell, rent, or refinance the inherited property, you need to know whether it must go through probate. In Florida, probate is the court-supervised process of validating a will, paying debts, and distributing assets to heirs.

Probate Is Usually Required If:

  • The property was solely in the deceased's name
  • The property was owned as a tenant in common
  • There is no valid will, or the will does not specify a transfer-on-death beneficiary

Probate May Be Avoided If:

  • The property was held in a living trust
  • The property was jointly owned with rights of survivorship
  • A transfer-on-death deed or lady bird deed was recorded

If probate is required, the process can take anywhere from a few months (for summary administration of small estates) to over a year (for formal administration). During this time, you may be limited in what you can do with the property. An experienced probate attorney in Brevard County can help you understand your rights and obligations.

Step 3: Understand the Financial Obligations

Inheriting a house isn't free. Even if the mortgage is paid off, ongoing costs add up quickly — and if there is a mortgage, those payments don't stop just because the owner passed away.

Mortgage and Debt Responsibilities

If the property has an outstanding mortgage, the loan remains due. Federal law allows heirs to assume certain types of mortgages, particularly those backed by FHA, VA, or USDA. However, conventional loans may require full repayment or refinancing. If the estate doesn't have sufficient assets to cover the mortgage, the lender may eventually initiate foreclosure if payments stop.

Other debts to consider include:

  • Home equity loans or lines of credit
  • Property taxes owed to Brevard County
  • Homeowners association (HOA) fees, which are common in Viera and planned communities
  • Outstanding contractor liens or utility bills

Ongoing Ownership Costs

While you decide what to do with the property, you'll need to budget for:

  • Property taxes (Brevard County's tax rate is approximately 0.9–1.2% of assessed value)
  • Insurance premiums, which may increase if the home loses its homestead exemption
  • Lawn care, pest control, and routine maintenance
  • Utilities and security monitoring

Step 4: Evaluate Your Options

Once the property is secure and you understand the legal and financial landscape, it's time to decide what to do with it. Most heirs choose one of three paths: keep the home, rent it out, or sell it.

Option 1: Move In

If the home is in good condition and located in a desirable area — like a waterfront property in Cocoa Beach or a quiet neighborhood in Viera — moving in may be the right choice. This is especially true if you can claim the property as your primary residence and benefit from Florida's homestead exemption, which reduces property taxes and protects the home from certain creditor claims.

However, be realistic about the home's condition. Older homes in Titusville or Cocoa may need significant updates to meet modern building codes and your personal standards.

Option 2: Rent It Out

Brevard County has a strong rental market, fueled by tourism, the space industry, and seasonal residents. Renting the property can generate steady income, but it also makes you a landlord — with all the responsibilities that entails.

Consider whether you're prepared to handle tenant screening, maintenance requests, property management fees, and Florida's landlord-tenant laws. If you live out of state, you'll likely need to hire a local property management company, which typically charges 8–12% of monthly rent.

Option 3: Sell the Property

For most heirs, selling is the simplest and cleanest solution. It converts a complex asset into cash that can be divided among beneficiaries, used to pay off estate debts, or reinvested as you see fit. The challenge is choosing the right sales method.

Step 5: Choose Your Selling Strategy

If you decide to sell, you have two main options: list the home traditionally with a real estate agent, or sell directly to a cash buyer.

Traditional Listing

Listing with a realtor makes sense if the home is in excellent condition, in a hot market, and you have time to wait for the right buyer. In areas like Melbourne and Palm Bay, well-maintained homes can sell quickly — but the process still takes 30–60 days on average, plus time for inspections, appraisals, and buyer financing.

The downsides? You'll pay 5–6% in commissions, cover closing costs, and may need to invest in repairs, staging, and professional photography. If the home is dated or has deferred maintenance, it may sit on the market for months while you continue paying holding costs.

Cash Sale

Selling to a cash buyer is the fastest, most hassle-free option — especially for inherited homes that need work. Cash buyers purchase properties as-is, meaning you don't need to lift a paintbrush, replace the roof, or haul away old furniture. The sale can close in as little as 7 to 14 days, and there are no agent commissions or hidden fees.

For heirs living out of state, managing a traditional sale from afar can be a logistical nightmare. A cash buyer eliminates the need for multiple trips to Brevard County, contractor negotiations, and open houses.

Step 6: Prepare for the Emotional Side of Inheriting

Don't underestimate the emotional weight of sorting through a loved one's possessions. A home in Cape Canaveral isn't just a building — it's a lifetime of memories, holidays, and family history. Give yourself permission to grieve, and don't feel rushed to clear everything out overnight.

Many cash buyers understand this and allow heirs to take what they want and leave the rest. You don't need to empty the house before selling. This small courtesy can make a big difference during a difficult time.

Step 7: Consult Professionals

Inheriting real estate involves legal, financial, and tax considerations that most people aren't equipped to handle alone. Build a team of professionals to guide you:

  • Probate Attorney: To navigate the court process and ensure proper transfer of title
  • CPA or Tax Advisor: To explain stepped-up basis, capital gains, and estate tax implications
  • Real Estate Professional or Cash Buyer: To evaluate the property and present selling options
  • Estate Liquidator: If the home contains valuable antiques, vehicles, or collectibles
