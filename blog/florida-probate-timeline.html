Florida Probate Timeline: How Long to Sell an Inherited Property

When a loved one passes away and leaves behind real estate in Brevard County — whether it's a coastal condo in Cocoa Beach, a family home in Melbourne, or a retirement property in Palm Bay — one of the first questions heirs ask is: How long until I can sell it?

The answer depends on several factors, including the type of probate required, the complexity of the estate, and whether all heirs agree on how to proceed. In this guide, we'll break down the Florida probate timeline step by step, explain what causes delays, and show you how to navigate the process as efficiently as possible.

Understanding Florida's Two Probate Paths

Florida offers two main probate processes: summary administration and formal administration. The path your loved one's estate takes will largely determine how long you're waiting before you can sell the inherited property.

Summary Administration: The Fast Track

Summary administration is a streamlined probate process designed for smaller estates or cases where the decedent has been deceased for more than two years. To qualify, the total value of the probate estate must be less than $75,000 (not counting exempt property like the homestead residence) or the decedent must have passed away more than two years ago.

Because summary administration bypasses many of the formal requirements of full probate — such as appointing a personal representative, filing inventories, and publishing extended creditor notices — it moves much more quickly.

Typical timeline: 2 to 5 months from filing to distribution.

For heirs in Brevard County eager to sell an inherited property, summary administration is the preferred route when available. Once the court issues the order of summary administration, title can be transferred and the property can be sold — sometimes within just a few months of the death.

Formal Administration: The Standard Process

Formal administration is required for larger estates and for most cases where the decedent has been deceased for less than two years. This is the full probate process and includes court oversight at every stage.

The formal administration timeline in Florida typically looks like this:

  • Weeks 1–2: File the petition, will, and death certificate with the circuit court. In Brevard County, this is done at the 18th Judicial Circuit Court in Viera.
  • Weeks 3–6: The court validates the will and issues letters of administration appointing the personal representative.
  • Weeks 7–10: The personal representative notifies creditors and publishes the required notice in a local newspaper, such as Florida Today.
  • Months 3–6: The creditor claims period runs (typically three months from the date of first publication). The personal representative also inventories assets, appraises real estate, and begins settling known debts.
  • Months 6–9: The personal representative files an accounting with the court, pays valid creditor claims, and petitions for authority to distribute assets.
  • Months 9–12: The court reviews the accounting and issues an order of discharge, allowing the estate to be closed and property to be distributed or sold.

Typical timeline: 6 to 12 months, though complex estates can take 18 months or longer.

Factors That Delay the Probate Process

Even within the general timelines above, certain factors can stretch the probate process far beyond what heirs expect. Understanding these pitfalls can help you avoid or mitigate them.

Will Contests and Family Disputes

When siblings, spouses, or other relatives disagree about the validity of the will or the distribution of assets, the resulting litigation can add months — or years — to probate. Even a single contested issue can trigger depositions, hearings, and appeals that paralyze the entire estate.

Unknown or Disputed Creditors

Florida requires creditors to be notified and given time to file claims. If unknown creditors emerge late in the process, or if the personal representative disputes the validity of a claim, the court must resolve the issue before the estate can close. This is especially common when the deceased owned a business or had significant unsecured debt.

Complex or Out-of-State Assets

If the estate includes property outside of Florida — such as a vacation home in Georgia or investment land in Alabama — ancillary probate may be required in those states as well. Each state's probate system operates independently, creating parallel timelines that must both conclude before the estate is fully settled.

Tax Complications

While most Florida estates do not owe state estate taxes, federal estate tax returns (Form 706) may be required for large estates. The IRS can take months to process these returns, and the estate cannot be closed until tax clearance is received. Additionally, if the decedent had complex investment income or unfiled tax returns, resolving these issues with the IRS can cause significant delays.

Difficulty Locating Heirs

If the will names heirs who are difficult to locate — or if the decedent died intestate and the court must identify distant relatives through genealogical research — probate can stall for months while search efforts continue.

Real Estate Market Conditions

While market conditions don't affect the probate timeline directly, they can influence how long it takes to sell the inherited property once court approval is granted. In slower markets, homes in areas like Titusville or Cocoa may sit unsold for months, extending the overall process of settling the estate.

Can You Sell a House While It's in Probate?

Yes — but with important caveats. Whether you can sell during probate depends on the type of administration and the authority granted by the court.

Selling During Summary Administration

Because summary administration does not appoint a personal representative, all heirs must agree to the sale and sign the necessary documents. If heirs are in agreement, the property can be listed and sold once the court issues the order of summary administration. In practice, this means most summary administration sales happen after the court order is entered, though some buyers may make contingent offers earlier.

Selling During Formal Administration

In formal administration, the personal representative's authority determines whether court approval is needed for a sale.

  • Unlimited authority: If the will grants unlimited powers, or if the court issues letters of administration without restriction, the personal representative can sell real estate without a separate court hearing.
  • Limited authority: If authority is limited, the representative must file a petition to sell real property and obtain court approval before listing or accepting an offer. This adds several weeks to the timeline.

Experienced cash buyers who work with probate properties understand these requirements and can coordinate with the personal representative and the court to ensure a smooth transaction.

No Buyer Financing Contingencies

Strategies to Expedite Florida Probate

If you're navigating probate in Brevard County and want to move as quickly as possible, consider these strategies:

  • Hire an experienced probate attorney. A lawyer who knows the 18th Judicial Circuit Court can help you avoid paperwork errors and procedural delays.
  • Communicate proactively with heirs. Get all beneficiaries on the same page early to prevent disputes later.
  • Organize financial documents. Gather bank statements, tax returns, property deeds, and debt information before filing the petition.
  • Publish creditor notices promptly. The three-month creditor clock doesn't start until publication, so file the notice as soon as you have letters of administration.
  • Request unlimited authority. If you're the personal representative, ask the court for full powers so you can sell real estate without additional hearings.
  • Get a cash offer early. Even if you can't close immediately, having a buyer lined up means no gap between court approval and closing.

Probate in Florida takes time — there's no way around it. But understanding the timeline helps you set realistic expectations, make informed decisions, and avoid the frustration of unexpected delays. Whether your loved one's estate qualifies for the fast track of summary administration or requires the full formal process, you have options for moving forward efficiently.
