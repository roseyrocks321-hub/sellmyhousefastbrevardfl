Inherited Property Taxes in Florida: What Heirs Must Know

Inheriting a home in Florida can feel like a gift and a burden at the same time. Whether you have inherited a beach condo in Cocoa Beach, a mid-century ranch in Melbourne, or a canal-front property in Merritt Island, the tax implications of inherited real estate can significantly affect how much money you actually walk away with when you sell.

Florida is one of the most tax-friendly states for heirs, but that does not mean inherited property comes without obligations. From property tax reassessments to capital gains calculations, understanding the rules helps you make smarter decisions about whether to keep, rent, or sell the property.

This guide covers everything Florida heirs need to know about inherited property taxes, including the powerful step-up in basis rule, how Florida’s lack of an inheritance tax benefits you, and strategies for minimizing your tax burden when you decide to sell.

Florida Has No State Inheritance Tax—But That Is Not the Whole Story

Let us start with the good news. Florida does not impose a state inheritance tax or estate tax. Since the repeal of Florida’s estate tax in 2005, heirs generally do not owe state taxes simply for receiving property from a deceased relative.

However, federal estate tax may still apply if the total estate value exceeds the federal exemption threshold—$13.61 million per individual as of 2024. For the vast majority of Brevard County families, federal estate tax is not a concern. But the absence of inheritance tax does not mean you are off the hook entirely.

Taxes Heirs Still Need to Consider

  • Property taxes: The annual tax bill does not disappear when the owner passes away.
  • Capital gains tax: Owed on profit when you sell the inherited property, though step-up in basis usually minimizes this.
  • Income tax: If you rent the property, rental income is taxable.
  • Federal estate tax: Only applies to estates exceeding the federal exemption limit.

The key takeaway: while you will not pay an inheritance tax in Florida, selling the property triggers other tax considerations that every heir should understand.

The Step-Up in Basis: Your Biggest Tax Advantage

The step-up in basis is the single most important tax benefit for heirs selling inherited property in Florida. It is also one of the most misunderstood concepts in real estate taxation.

How Step-Up in Basis Works

When you inherit property, the IRS resets the property’s tax basis to its fair market value on the date of the original owner’s death. This is a huge advantage over receiving property as a gift during the owner’s lifetime, where you would inherit the original purchase price as your basis.

Here is a practical example. Imagine your parents bought a home in Palm Bay in 1985 for $65,000. By the time they passed away in 2024, the home was worth $350,000. If they had gifted it to you while alive, your basis would remain $65,000. If you sold it for $350,000, you would owe capital gains tax on $285,000 of profit.

But because you inherited it, your basis steps up to $350,000. If you sell it for $350,000, you owe zero capital gains tax. If you sell it for $370,000 after making some improvements, you only owe gains on the $20,000 difference.

"My mom passed away and left me her house in Rockledge. I was terrified I'd owe a fortune in taxes when I sold it. Then I learned about step-up in basis. Because she bought it decades ago, my tax basis was the current market value. I sold it to a cash buyer, paid almost nothing in taxes, and used the proceeds to pay off my own mortgage. I wish I had known how simple it would be."

Alternate Valuation Date Option

In some cases, the estate executor can elect to use the property’s value six months after the date of death rather than the death date itself. This alternate valuation date can be beneficial if the property declines in value during those six months, reducing potential capital gains for heirs. However, this election applies to the entire estate, not just one property, so it requires careful planning with a tax professional.

Property Tax Changes After Inheritance

One of the first surprises many heirs face is a property tax increase. In Florida, property taxes are governed by the Save Our Homes amendment, which caps annual assessment increases for homestead properties at 3% or the rate of inflation, whichever is lower.

Loss of Homestead Exemption and Cap

When the original owner passes away, the homestead exemption and Save Our Homes cap typically disappear. The property is reassessed at current market value, which can cause a dramatic spike in annual taxes—especially if the previous owner lived in the home for decades.

For example, a home in Viera that was taxed at an assessed value of $180,000 under the previous owner’s homestead cap might be reassessed at $320,000 after inheritance. At Brevard County’s approximate tax rate of 1% to 1.3%, that is a jump from roughly $1,800-$2,300 per year to $3,200-$4,100 per year.

Options to Reduce Property Taxes as an Heir

  • Apply for homestead exemption: If you move into the inherited property and make it your primary residence, you can apply for your own homestead exemption, which reduces the assessed value by up to $50,000.
  • Portability benefit: If you already own a homestead in Florida, you may be able to transfer some of your previous Save Our Homes benefit to the inherited property.
  • Challenge the assessment: If the reassessed value seems too high, you can file an appeal with the Brevard County Property Appraiser’s office.

If you do not plan to live in the home, these exemptions will not apply, and you will pay taxes at the full market-assessed rate. This is one reason many heirs choose to sell quickly rather than hold inherited property as a rental or second home.

Capital Gains Tax When Selling Inherited Property

When you sell inherited property, any profit above your stepped-up basis is subject to capital gains tax. The rate depends on how long you hold the property and your overall income level.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term Capital Gains

Inherited property is automatically treated as long-term for capital gains purposes, regardless of how long you actually owned it. This means you benefit from the lower long-term capital gains rates, which range from 0% to 20% depending on your income bracket, plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax for high earners.

Calculating Your Taxable Gain

To determine your taxable gain:

  • Start with the sale price.
  • Subtract the stepped-up basis (market value at death).
  • Subtract selling expenses (realtor commissions, closing costs, repairs made specifically for sale).
  • The remainder is your taxable capital gain.

For most heirs in Brevard County who sell promptly after inheritance, the taxable gain is minimal or zero—especially if the local market has been relatively stable since the date of death.

Depreciation Recapture for Rental Properties

If the previous owner rented out the property and claimed depreciation, or if you rent it before selling, you may owe depreciation recapture tax at a flat 25% rate on the depreciation amount. This is a complex area where consulting a CPA is strongly recommended.

Selling Strategies to Minimize Tax Burden

Even with Florida’s favorable tax climate, strategic timing and structure can help you keep more of the proceeds from an inherited home sale.

Sell Promptly After Inheritance

The simplest strategy is to sell as soon as probate or estate administration allows. Because your basis is stepped up to the date-of-death value, selling quickly usually means little to no appreciation has occurred, resulting in minimal capital gains. This also avoids months of property tax bills, insurance, maintenance, and utility costs.

Consider a 1031 Exchange (If Keeping Investment Property)

If you want to keep the real estate investment but not the specific property, a 1031 exchange allows you to defer capital gains by reinvesting proceeds into a like-kind property. However, 1031 exchanges come with strict timelines—45 days to identify a replacement property and 180 days to close—and are only available for investment properties, not personal residences.

Distribute to Multiple Heirs Before Sale

If the estate has multiple beneficiaries, consider distributing the property to the heirs before selling. Each heir receives their own stepped-up basis and can use their individual capital gains exemptions if they later sell. However, this strategy requires careful coordination and legal documentation to avoid disputes.

Common Mistakes Heirs Make with Inherited Property

After years of helping Brevard County homeowners, we have seen the same costly mistakes repeated by well-meaning heirs:

  • Waiting too long to sell: Holding costs accumulate, and market conditions can change.
  • Assuming they owe massive taxes: Many heirs panic unnecessarily because they do not understand step-up in basis.
  • Not challenging a high tax assessment: The Property Appraiser’s valuation is not always accurate.
  • Neglecting to secure the property: Vacant homes attract vandalism, squatters, and water damage.
  • Trying to manage a remote renovation: Out-of-state heirs often lose money on contractors and delays.

The best approach is usually to get accurate information quickly, consult a tax professional if the estate is large, and make a decision based on facts rather than fear.

Key Takeaways for Florida Heirs

Inheriting property in Brevard County does not have to be overwhelming. Florida’s lack of inheritance tax, combined with the federal step-up in basis rule, creates a favorable environment for heirs who choose to sell.

  • You do not owe Florida inheritance tax on the property you receive.
  • Your tax basis steps up to the market value at the date of death, minimizing capital gains.
  • Property taxes may increase after inheritance due to loss of homestead cap and exemptions.
  • Selling quickly is usually the most tax-efficient and cost-effective strategy.
  • A local cash buyer can close fast, eliminate holding costs, and simplify the process.
